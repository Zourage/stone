# Science tasks. Each: explain at the level of a strong university student, 20-35 sentences, with the real technical terms, quantities and units, the causal chain step by step, and one common misconception corrected.

S01 immunology: How the adaptive immune system responds to a vaccine: antigen uptake and presentation on MHC, activation of helper T cells, B cell activation, germinal centres and affinity maturation, class switching, memory cells, and why a second dose gives a faster and stronger response.

S02 kidney: How one nephron makes urine: filtration at the glomerulus and what drives it, reabsorption in the proximal tubule, the countercurrent mechanism in the loop of Henle, and how ADH and aldosterone adjust water and salt. Include typical numbers (filtration rate, urine volume).

S03 nerve and muscle: From a nerve impulse to a muscle contraction: resting potential, the action potential and its ion channels, transmission at the neuromuscular junction, calcium release, and the sliding-filament cycle with ATP. Say what happens when ATP runs out.

S04 blood and oxygen: How haemoglobin carries oxygen: cooperative binding and the S-shaped curve, the Bohr effect, the role of 2,3-BPG, how carbon dioxide is carried, and what changes at high altitude and in carbon monoxide poisoning.

S05 organic mechanisms: Compare the SN1 and SN2 reaction mechanisms: rate laws, intermediates and transition states, stereochemical outcome, the effect of substrate structure, nucleophile, leaving group and solvent, and how to predict which one dominates for a given case. Work through two examples.

S06 bonding: Use molecular orbital theory to explain why the oxygen molecule is paramagnetic and has a double bond, why the helium dimer does not exist, and how bond order relates to bond length and energy. Contrast with what the simple electron-pair picture predicts.

S07 equilibrium: Explain Gibbs free energy, its relation to enthalpy, entropy and the equilibrium constant, and apply it to the industrial synthesis of ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen: why high pressure, why a compromise temperature, what the catalyst does and does not change.

S08 spectroscopy: Explain how proton NMR spectroscopy works and how to read a spectrum: nuclear spin in a magnetic field, chemical shift, integration, spin-spin splitting and the n+1 rule. Then deduce the structure of ethanol from a described spectrum.

S09 relativity: Explain why clocks on navigation satellites run at a different rate from clocks on the ground: the special-relativity effect, the general-relativity effect, their signs and rough sizes per day, the net effect, and what position error would build up if it were ignored.

S10 quantum: Explain the double-slit experiment with single electrons: what is observed, why neither a pure particle nor a pure wave picture fits, what measuring the path does, and what the uncertainty relation does and does not say.

S11 molecular biology: Explain the polymerase chain reaction: its ingredients, the three temperature steps and what happens in each, why the amount doubles each cycle, how many copies after 30 cycles, and two ways it can fail.

S12 consensus: Explain how a group of computers can agree on a sequence of commands when some may crash and messages may be lost (a leader-based consensus protocol such as Raft): terms, leader election, log replication, commitment, and why a majority is needed. Give a scenario where a partition heals.

S13 complexity: Explain the question whether P equals NP: what the two classes are, what a reduction is, what NP-complete means, one worked reduction in outline, and what would follow in practice if the answer were yes.

S14 public-key cryptography: Explain how RSA works with a small worked example (two small primes, the public and private exponents, encrypting and decrypting one number), why it is believed hard to break, and two ways real systems go wrong.

S15 memory: Explain virtual memory in an operating system: pages, page tables, the translation cache, page faults, swapping, and why a program can slow down a hundredfold when memory runs short. Define the page-lookup procedure in the logic notation.

S16 early-2000s worms: Tell the history of the big internet worms of 2000 to 2004 as you recall it (the love-letter email worm, Code Red, SQL Slammer, Blaster, Sasser): for each, the year, how it spread in general terms, how fast, the damage, and what changed in software practice afterwards. Mark what you are sure of and what you are not. History and lessons only; no technical detail beyond what a history book would give.

S17 the 2000s internet: Describe how the internet changed between 2000 and 2009: the dot-com crash, file sharing and the lawsuits around it, broadband replacing dial-up, the rise of search advertising, blogs and social networks, the first smartphones. Give dates and names where you recall them and say how confident you are.

S18 algebra: Define a group, give three examples and one non-example, prove that the identity element is unique and that each element has exactly one inverse, and state Lagrange's theorem with an example.

S19 evolution: Explain how antibiotic resistance spreads in a population of bacteria by natural selection: variation, selection pressure, inheritance, horizontal gene transfer, the cost of resistance, and why finishing or shortening a course matters. Include a small numerical example of allele frequency over generations.

S20 climate: Explain the greenhouse effect quantitatively: the energy balance of the Earth, the temperature without an atmosphere, which gases absorb and why, what doubling carbon dioxide does, and two feedbacks with their direction.

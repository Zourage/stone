# Dictionary probe 1 — 2026-09-15

Corpus 1295 sentences, lexicon 206 entries, at `e7db108`. Nothing was added to
the corpus or the lexicon by this run. It is a probe.

**Result: coverage of ordinary conversational English falls from 67% at the top
of the frequency list to 27% by rank 800, and the toki-pona strategy of building
a missing word out of existing roots is not available to this language —
two independent readers used it 6 times in 800 words.** The constructions do
real work (66 words need no vocabulary at all); compounding does none.

## Why this test exists

Coverage test 1 (D121) used 60 blind sentences written to a domain description
**I wrote**, and the equipment band collapsed partly because I put "physical
equipment" in the prompt. Author bias sat inside the measurement. A frequency
list has no opinion about what the language ought to cover.

It also answers a question the sentence test only answers incidentally: not
*is this word missing* but **how does the language solve it** — root, derivation,
construction, compound, or not at all. That is the toki pona question made
countable.

## Protocol

1. **Word list.** OpenSubtitles 2018 English via hermitdave/FrequencyWords,
   CC-BY-SA — deliberately the same source and license as `data/ko_frequency.json`,
   and conversational register rather than web text, which fits D1's two people
   talking. Taken in frequency order.
2. **Filtering, all of it auditable.** An explicit stop list of pure function
   words the grammar handles by construction — articles, the pronoun paradigm,
   be/have/do, the modals, conjunctions, the prepositions that map to case
   markers and relational words — written out as a literal rather than hidden in
   a regex. Then a mechanical lemmatizer (regular suffixes plus an irregular
   table) so the probe counts concepts, not English morphology: `went` and `gone`
   must not each score as a miss against a language with no inflection. Top 800
   survivors, in four bands of 200.
3. **Eight cold agents, two independent arms per band**, each with
   `spec/grammar.md`, the complete lexicon as form/pos/gloss/notes,
   `lexicon/README.md` and `corpus/README.md`. The lexicon README is not
   optional: its "Not coined on purpose" list is what separates a real gap from a
   concept the constructions already cover, and a reader without it calls
   *always* and *nobody* gaps.
4. **Verdicts:** ROOT / DERIVED / CONSTRUCTION / COMPOUND / GAP / INFLECTION.
   COMPOUND is strict by instruction — two roots count only if a reader lands on
   the right meaning, and *small machine* is named in the prompt as a GAP for
   screwdriver rather than a compound.
5. **Every GAP gets a scope call**, IN_SCOPE or OUT_OF_SCOPE against D1 and D34.
   A subtitle corpus is film dialogue; without this the number is meaningless.

Two arms per band for the same reason as D121: tolerance for "covered" *is* the
measurement, and one reader's tolerance is unfalsifiable.

## How the solved words are solved

| | count |
|---|---|
| ROOT | 252 |
| CONSTRUCTION | 66 |
| DERIVED | 28 |
| **COMPOUND** | **2** |

Across all 800 words, only six were compounded by either arm: tomorrow,
yesterday, office, address, lucky, busy.

**This is the finding.** Both readers had the grammar's compound rule, the
lexicon README stating that the language builds what it lacks, and an explicit
instruction that compounding was encouraged. They used constructions 66 times —
the same build-from-parts instinct, applied where it works — and compounded six.

The reason is the one D107 half-stated and did not follow through on. Toki pona's
`ilo nanpa` works because `ilo` is vague: tool, device, any made thing, loose
enough that number-tool intersects onto computer. D12 and D35 built this
language's originality on the opposite — three roots for knowing, three for
error, three for asking. **Sharp roots do not stack.** The machine root means
machine, so machine + small is not an underspecified screwdriver, it is a false
statement about a machine. Compounding here is not an underused tool; the
capacity for it was spent at the founding.

Adopting the toki pona strategy would therefore mean coining first: a small set
of deliberately generic roots whose job is to be compound heads. That is a
coining decision against D35, not a policy change, and it is not made here.

## The 76 in-scope gaps, grouped

**No social register at all** — sorry, thank, welcome, bye, goodbye, appreciate,
blame, excuse. D1 says two people learn this in their heads and talk in it. They
cannot apologize, thank each other, or say goodbye.

**No quantitative hedging** — enough, almost, half, quite, rather, about, even,
especially, fit, million. The language has four obligatory evidentials and six
stances for *epistemic* precision and nothing at all for *approximation*. For
engineering and science talk (D34), being able to say "I infer it, and I propose
it rather than claim it" but not "almost" or "roughly half" is a real imbalance,
and it is the most surprising thing this run found.

**Bodily and daily life** — eat, drink, sleep, food, water, dinner, lunch, tea,
coffee, hungry, sick, tired, wake, morning, afternoon, summer.

**Workplace-social** — team, boss, company, partner, office, service, public,
private, meet, phone, contact, information, situation, experience.

**Plain functional holes** — like, around, through, forward, push, pull, return,
touch, cover, clean, strong, careful, normal, free, rest, green, table, age.

The middle two groups are coverage test 1's scheduling band at scale: **the
corpus talks about the work and never about the situation the work happens in.**
That is a narrowness in situation, not in domain, and it is inside D34.

## A separate category this run turned up: the teaching gap

Both arms called *hundred* and *thousand* gaps. By the stated rule they are not —
grammar line 47 says a numeral before ten multiplies, s0103 is
`two-ten-five-one` for twenty-six, so ten-before-ten gives a hundred. **The rule
covers it and the corpus never shows it, so a cold reader cannot know.** That is
neither a lexical gap nor a grammar gap but a third thing: a rule stated and
never taught. Worth watching for, because the acceptance test cannot see it
either — it only ever asks about sentences the corpus contains.

## Verdict

No bar, same as D121. What it establishes:

1. Coverage of ordinary conversation is 67% at the top of the frequency list and
   falls to 27% by rank 800.
2. **Compounding is not available to this language at anything like toki pona's
   rate, and the cause is D35's carving.** Six uses in 800 words by two readers
   who were told to use it.
3. Constructions carry 66 words with no vocabulary at all, which is where the
   build-from-parts instinct does work here.
4. 76 in-scope gaps, dominated by two registers the corpus has never written in:
   the social and the approximate.
5. A third gap category exists — a rule the grammar states and the corpus never
   demonstrates.

Re-running this needs no fresh draw; the frequency list is fixed and the point is
that the number moves when the lexicon does. Re-run it after any coining round.

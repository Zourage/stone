# Coverage test 1 — 2026-09-15

Corpus 1295 sentences, lexicon 206 entries, at `ddc35e2`.

**Result: the grammar reaches, the lexicon does not. Two cold translators holding
the full materials produced a well-formed sentence for 39 and 38 of 60 blind
English sentences and were blocked on a missing word for the rest. Neither was
blocked on grammar once.** Blocks are concentrated almost entirely in two bands:
physical equipment, where the language has essentially no vocabulary at all, and
the calendar, where it has units but no names.

## Why this test exists

The acceptance test cannot measure reach. Its held set is drawn from
`corpus/corpus.jsonl`, so every sentence it scores was written by someone who
already had the lexicon open; a sentence that could not be said was never
written down and so is never scored. Test 5 passing at 97.5% says the corpus
teaches the language well (D3, D50). It says nothing about whether the language
covers its own stated purpose (D1, D34).

This test inverts the deprivation. The acceptance test starves the *translator*
and uses corpus sentences as the source. The coverage test starves the *source*
and gives the translator everything, which is the normal translation protocol
(D72).

## Protocol

1. **Blind source.** One cold agent, given a one-paragraph domain description
   — engineers at a bench: code, experiments, physical equipment, scheduling,
   disagreement — and nothing else. It never saw the lexicon, the grammar, the
   spec or the corpus, and was not told what the language can do. It wrote 60
   English sentences, 12 per band, kept verbatim in
   `tests/fixtures/coverage_2026-09-15.txt`.
2. **Full-materials translators.** Two cold agents, independently, each given
   `spec/grammar.md`, the complete 206-entry lexicon as form/pos/gloss/notes,
   and 200 randomly drawn train pairs. Told to paraphrase freely where the
   meaning survives, told that compounding is allowed, told explicitly that
   stretching a root well past its gloss is a block and not a paraphrase, and
   forbidden to invent a form.
3. **Verdicts.** Each sentence gets one of OK, CLUMSY (said, but long-winded or
   lossy — what is lost recorded), BLOCKED_WORD (missing words listed) or
   BLOCKED_GRAMMAR (missing construction described).
4. **Well-formedness.** Every sentence either translator produced is parsed by
   `stonelib.parse_sentence`. A verdict of OK on an unparseable string is not a
   coverage result.

Two translators rather than one because a single agent's tolerance for
paraphrase is the whole measurement. Their agreement is the confidence interval.

## By band — the actual finding

| band (12 each) | A sayable | B sayable | both blocked |
|---|---|---|---|
| code | 11 | 10 | 1 |
| experiments | 9 | 8 | 3 |
| **physical equipment** | **1** | **2** | **10** |
| scheduling | 6 | 7 | 4 |
| **discussion, disagreement** | **12** | **11** | **0** |

Discussion is complete and code and science talk are close to it, which is what
D1 and D34 said the language is for. Physical equipment is near-total failure.
Scheduling is half-built.

The two failing bands fail for different reasons and only one is a gap.

**Physical equipment is a scope boundary, not a gap.** The lexicon holds ~170
content roots and almost no nameable physical objects: machine, file, message,
line, thing, place is close to the entire inventory. Nothing about screwdrivers,
cables, valves, pumps or brackets was ever meant to be sayable. Both arms
refused to stretch the machine root to cover a screwdriver, which is the
behaviour the instructions asked for and the right call.

**Scheduling is a gap, and it is inside the stated purpose.** Four of the six
blocked lines there are blocked on nothing but calendar names. D85 coined week,
month, year, hour and minute; it did not coin the seven weekdays, any part of
day other than night, or holiday. The language can say "every hour" and "next
week" and cannot say "Monday morning". Two people coordinating work is the
stated use, and this is the ordinary vocabulary of it.

## Words both arms named as missing (23)

bent, box, bracket, branch, cable, clamp, corner, door, drift, fan, holiday,
leak, left, loose, lunch, pressure, pump, screwdriver, seal, shop, spare, valve,
voltage

Named by one arm only (20): afternoon, bearing, double, driver, drop, eat/meal,
fitting(s), Friday, half, lose, model, Monday, morning, order (goods), peak,
Thursday, touch, wire/wiring.

Three of these are worth separating from the rest because they are not physical
objects and not calendar names:

- **branch** — the version-control sense. Both arms found `condition, branch`
  and both correctly refused it: D35 carved that root as *the branch point in
  code*, a conditional, and the sense_notes say so. The carve worked; the
  concept is simply absent.
- **left** — the language has up, down, inside, outside, near and far, and no
  lateral axis at all. Not noticed before because no corpus sentence needed one.
- **half** — no fractions of any kind. Both arms reached for "a part of".

## What was expected to break and did not

The grammar. Going in, first-person plural looked like a hole; D64 builds it as
I + you + with and 36 corpus sentences use it. Third person falls out of the
that-one pronoun or the reported evidential. Indefinite "someone" is the bare
person root. Neither arm reported a single missing construction across 60
sentences written by someone who had never seen the grammar. The two
constructions either arm wanted — "rather X than Y" and suspended judgement
between believing and doubting — were both filed CLUMSY, said at double length,
not blocked.

## Corrections to the record this run forces

**"The lexicon is not the bottleneck" was true only of a closed set.** It was
measured on the corpus's own English, where 206 entries cover 1295 sentences and
all 31 thin features closed with zero new roots. On novel English in the same
register the lexicon is the only bottleneck there is. A count over a vocabulary
chosen by people holding that vocabulary measures nothing about reach — the same
error in a different instrument as D112's, where a count per feature was read as
a count per construction.

**A gloss-string lookup is not a coverage measure.** The first pass at this used
`compose.py`'s reverse index and reported 105 of 197 distinct words missing. The
number is wrong and is discarded. The index matches glosses, so it reported gaps
for error (mistake/fault/failure), fresh (new), row (line), blank (empty), heat
(hot) and broken (fix, done) — all of which have roots. It was caught by reading
the list rather than the total.

## Verdict

Not a pass/fail test and no bar is set for it. What it establishes:

1. The grammar covers its purpose domain. Zero construction gaps in 60 blind
   sentences, two independent readers.
2. Every sentence produced is well formed, 77 of 77. The paraphrases are real
   sentences, not hand-waving.
3. The lexicon covers discussion, code and experiment talk and does not cover
   physical objects, which is by design, or the calendar, which is not.
4. The one concrete thing to fix is calendar naming: seven weekdays and the
   parts of a day, both squarely inside D1 and D34.

Re-running this test means a fresh blind draw. Reusing
`coverage_2026-09-15.txt` after coining against it measures the coining, not the
language.
